Where the supply side fails poor pupils: lost class time, inexperienced staff and thin resources, with the biggest gaps between provinces

Part A looks at the supply side the teacher statutes can change (teachers, time in class, school leadership, classroom resources): where it is weakest, whether it is worse in poorer schools, and whether it goes with lower results. Part B (the earlier analysis) sets this against the demand side, who the pupils are. Data: Aprender 2025 (grade 6 census, pupil, teacher and director questionnaires), ERCE 2019 (grades 3 and 6), PISA 2018 (age 15), MICS 2019–20 (households).

  • Lost class time is the supply problem that hits poor pupils hardest and goes most clearly with lower learning. In ERCE, pupils in the poorest fifth of schools are more likely to say their teachers miss classes (49% vs 35%) or arrive late (31% vs 20%), and both are linked to scores 0.2–0.3 SD lower. In MICS, 43% of the poorest children (vs 24% of the richest) missed class because the teacher was absent, and 70% (vs 37%) had their school closed by a teacher strike.
  • Within a province, public schools serving poor and rich pupils get similar teachers on paper (tenure, contracts, experience, degrees): gaps of 0–9 percentage points, explaining under a tenth of the poor–rich score gap. Province-wide staffing rules spread these fairly evenly.
  • The large supply differences are between provinces: teachers not tenured range from about 20% to 90% across provinces, substitutes 14–66%, teachers reporting too few class days 10–50%, directors reporting class suspensions 1–80%. Patagonia (Santa Cruz, Tierra del Fuego, Neuquén, Chubut, Río Negro) combines very unstable staffing with the most lost class days; the poorer north lacks devices and connectivity. Provinces with more lost class days, more teachers split across schools, untenured directors and poor classroom resources score lower at the same SES. Since statutes are provincial, this is where statute reform can be targeted.
  • Poor schools' other disadvantages are resources, not contracts: insufficient materials (79% vs 17% of ERCE directors), school days of 4 hours or less (70% vs 33%), no devices or internet. These need funding and programmes alongside the statute.
  • Statute levers that reach the problems: substitution speed, leave and attendance rules (dimension D) for lost class time; entry, induction and transfer rules (A) for inexperienced and transient staff; appointment by full post (D6) for multi-school teachers; regular director contests (B1). Teacher contract type and degrees on their own predict little.

A1. Which supply factors go with lower results? Regression across public schools (Aprender 2025)

What is estimated. One regression per column, across public primary schools.

  • Outcome (left-hand side): the school's average grade-6 score (mean of language and maths), in standard deviations of pupil scores. 1 SD ≈ 77 points in language and 92 in maths on the Aprender scale, so 0.10 SD ≈ 8–9 Aprender points.
  • Supply factor (right-hand side): a share from 0 to 1, e.g. the share of the school's responding teachers who are substitutes, or 0/1 for the director.
  • Coefficient: the expected difference in the school's average score between a school where none and a school where all of its respondents report the factor. Negative = lower results.
  • Held constant in every column: the school's average pupil SES, rural location, and province (schools are only compared with schools in the same province).
  • Column (1): each factor in its own regression. Column (2): all factors in one regression, so each coefficient is net of the other factors.

Weighted by tested pupils; heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors in parentheses. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001. Missing answers are mean-filled with a missing flag (not shown). Associations, not effects.

Reading. Once intake and province are held constant, most supply factors have small links with results, and they are much smaller than the link with pupil SES. In order of size (column 1):

  • Teaching practice is the largest: a school where all pupils say their teachers do not usually explain until they understand scores 0.75 SD lower than one where none say so, i.e. about 0.075 SD (≈6 points) for every 10 percentage points more pupils saying it. It barely changes when all other factors are added (column 2).
  • Classroom climate and conditions: pupils unable to concentrate for noise (−0.11), dirty toilets (−0.11, but not once other factors are included), teachers reporting frequent interruptions (−0.06) or classes too big (−0.06).
  • Resources: no computers or tablets (−0.06; still −0.04 in column 2), lacking teaching materials (−0.05), poor furniture (−0.05), no internet (−0.02 to −0.03).
  • Time in class and staff stability: class suspensions (−0.04), too few class hours (−0.03), teachers new to teaching or to the school (−0.03 each; both hold in column 2).
  • No link: teachers' contract status (substitute, not tenured), degree, training, other jobs, director tenure.

All coefficients are for a school where every respondent reports the factor vs one where none does; real schools sit in between, so the typical difference between two schools is a fraction of these numbers.

Where are these factors worse? Poorest vs richest public schools

Reading. Within a province, most gaps between the poorest and richest public schools are small (0–9 percentage points), and together the supply factors account for only of the SD gap in results between those schools. Public-school staffing is allocated by province-wide rules (contests, lists, transfers), which spreads tenure, experience and contract types fairly evenly across a province's schools. The exceptions are devices, internet and teaching materials (+7 to +9 points in poorer schools) and teaching practice (+5).

A2. The big supply differences are between provinces

Province averages over public primary schools (Aprender 2025), provinces ordered from poorest to richest average pupil SES. Darker = more of the deficit (scaled within each column). Score: average grade-6 language and maths result (SD) of the province's public schools.

Regression across the 24 provinces

Outcome: the province's average public-school score (SD, as in A1). Supply factor: the province average, in percentage points. Coefficient: difference in the province's average score for 10 percentage points more of the factor, holding the province's average pupil SES constant. One regression per factor (24 provinces each). Province SES alone explains of the differences between provinces.

Ordinary least squares, 24 provinces; standard errors in parentheses; * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001. With 24 provinces, single significant results should be read with care.

Reading. The share of teachers who are not tenured ranges from about 20% to 90% across provinces, substitutes from 14% to 66%, teachers reporting too few class days from 10% to 50%, and directors reporting class suspensions as a problem from 1% to 80%. Two different problems stand out. Patagonia has a staffing and lost-time crisis: in Santa Cruz 90% of responding public-school teachers are not tenured, 66% are substitutes, 47% report too few class days and half of directors report class suspensions; Tierra del Fuego (79%, 53%, 50%, 80%), Neuquén (85%, 58%, 26%, 24%), Chubut and Río Negro follow. The poorer north has a resource gap: no computers or tablets for pupils in 79% of Chaco's schools, 72% of Formosa's and 69% of Tucumán's and Salta's, and no internet in the classroom in 86% (Tucumán) and 81% (Salta). Untenured directors are common everywhere: above 75% of public-school directors in half the provinces, and below 50% only in CABA (41%) and San Luis (44%). Across provinces, lacking teaching materials, internet in class, adequate classrooms and enough class days, and having teachers spread over several schools and directors without tenure, go with lower results at the same average SES.

A3. Teacher absence, materials and the length of the school day are where poor schools fall behind (ERCE 2019)

Reading. Pupils in the poorest fifth of schools are much more likely to say their teachers miss classes (49% vs 35%) and arrive late (31% vs 20%), and both go with clearly lower results. Directors of the poorest schools report insufficient teaching materials four times as often (79% vs 17%), and 70% of those schools run a school day of 4 hours or less (vs 33%). Teachers with 5 years or less of experience go with lower scores; contract type does not, once the school is held constant.

Targeting. (1) Time in class is the supply problem that is both worst in poor schools and most clearly linked to learning, and it is the one the statute governs most directly (substitution, leave, attendance). (2) Because public staffing is fairly uniform within provinces, the statute differences that matter show up between provinces: the Patagonian provinces (Santa Cruz, Tierra del Fuego, Neuquén, Chubut, Río Negro) for staffing stability and lost class days, and most provinces for director appointment by contest (see A2). (3) Poor schools' extra disadvantages in materials, devices and school-day length need funding and programmes, not statute changes. All results are associations, not effects.

A6. How much of these supply problems can the statutes explain?

Correction pending: see the note at the start of the evidence.

Statute rules differ only between provinces, so they can only explain the part of a supply problem that differs between provinces. Two steps: first, the share of the school-level variation in each problem that lies between provinces (the ceiling for any provincial law); second, how much of the between-province differences the coded rules explain.

Summary: the ceiling, and what the relevant rules explain

Between provinces: share of the variation across public schools that lies between province averages (R² of province fixed effects). Rules that should matter: the coded rules that, by the statute's own logic, act on the problem (entered together as 0/1 dummies). R², SES only → with rules: share of the differences between the 24 province averages explained by province SES alone, then with those rules added. Joint p: F-test that the rules add nothing. Most the statute could explain = between-province share × the best adjusted R² of any statute model (rules, indices or best single rule), an upper bound for this data. Blank rules = the coded statutes have no rule aimed at the problem (resources).

Regression table: each statute rule as a 0/1 dummy

Each cell is a separate regression across the provinces coded on that rule. Outcome: the province's average share of the supply problem among its public schools, in percentage points (0–100). Regressors: the statute dummy (1 = the province's statute has the rule; numeric rules split at the cut shown) and the province's average pupil SES. Coefficient: difference in percentage points between provinces with and without the rule, at the same average SES; standard error in parentheses. Column headings show how many provinces have the rule / are coded. Rules with fewer than 3 provinces on either side are not tested (A1, A4, A5, B2, B3, B4, C2, C4–C6).

OLS, 21–24 provinces per cell; * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001.

Reading.

  • The ceiling is low. For most supply problems only 2–15% of the variation across schools is between provinces; the rest is between schools in the same province, which a provincial statute applies equally to. The exceptions are devices (27%), internet in class (14%) and the share of untenured teachers (15%).
  • The rules that should matter explain little of the between-province differences. None of the theory-matched sets is jointly significant: the four rules on substitution, leave, attendance and absence sanctions add 12 points of R² for "too few class days" (p = 0.49) and 13 for "too few class hours" (p = 0.18); entry and transfer rules add 5–6 points for new and inexperienced teachers (p ≈ 0.5).
  • A few dummies line up with the mechanism they should have: provinces that appoint secondary teachers by full post (D6, 4 provinces) have 17 points fewer teachers working in a second school; provinces that wait more than 3 days to appoint a substitute (D1) have 14 points fewer substitutes, which also means more days without a teacher; where teachers or unions choose most of the ranking board (C3), 16 points more directors are untenured.
  • Other significant cells are markers, not mechanisms: an entry age cap (A2) goes with worse internet, materials and class days, which no age rule can cause; it identifies a group of provinces. With 276 tests, about 14 would pass p < 0.05 by chance; 28 did.
  • So, in general: the coded statutes can account for at most a few percent of the variation in most supply problems that matter for learning (under 5% for all but two: about 6% for internet in the classroom and up to 14% for devices, where the bound rests on one rule, the remote-school bonus, coded in only 17 provinces and with no plausible link to devices). Statute reform should be argued on the specific mechanisms above (full-post appointment, substitution rules, director selection) and on the province-level targeting in A2, not on the expectation that statute differences explain today's supply gaps.

B4. The predictors that matter, compared across datasets

How to read the comparison

  • Family factors repeat across all three assessments and across ages: repetition, preschool, books, indigenous background and the maths gender gap are the most stable findings in the report.
  • Peer SES (the school's average family background) is the strongest school-level predictor everywhere; its units differ by dataset (Aprender's index has a wider spread), so compare signs and significance, not sizes.
  • Rural schools score slightly higher once family background is held constant: the raw urban–rural gap is a family-background gap.
  • Teacher contract, experience, degree and training are close to zero in Aprender (360,000 pupils, so even 0.02 SD is significant) and mostly not significant in ERCE; the exceptions are teachers with 5 years or less of experience in grade-3 maths (−0.18 SD) and a teaching qualification in grade-6 maths (+0.45 SD; few teachers lack one). Teacher absence is the teacher factor with consistent, sizeable links.
  • Within a model full of related measures, single coefficients can take unexpected signs (for example, home internet or a computer in Aprender, child work including chores in ERCE grade 6). Read them as "holding the other assets constant", not as the effect of providing internet.

B7. What the demand side means for the statute

  • The statute levers that work through contract status, degrees or training hours are unlikely, on this evidence, to move learning on their own: these teacher characteristics predict little once family and school are taken into account.
  • The teacher factor with a consistent link is whether teachers are in the classroom (absence, lateness, strike days), and it falls hardest on the poorest pupils. Statute rules on leave, substitution speed and attendance (dimension D) are the closest levers.
  • Because who the pupils are (family and peer SES) explains far more than any school or teacher variable, any comparison of provinces or schools should adjust for intake, as the province explorer's SES-expected measure does.
  • All results are associations in cross-sections: they describe who scores higher, not what would happen if a factor changed.

B8. Methods and caveats

Models. One weighted linear regression per dataset × grade × subject, outcome standardised to mean 0 and SD 1 (weighted, within the analysed sample). All four groups of predictors enter together; categorical predictors as dummies against a base; missing answers kept as their own category (numeric: mean-filled plus a missing flag) so that no pupil is dropped for one unanswered item. Geography includes province fixed effects (Aprender) or region fixed effects (PISA). Standard errors clustered by school. ERCE and PISA: every plausible value estimated separately and combined with Rubin's rules (school-clustered errors approximate the official replicate-weight errors). Weights: Aprender subject expansion weights; ERCE normalised weight WS; PISA final student weight.

Data notes. (1) Aprender 2025's pupil SES index (indice) is coded higher = poorer in the source (quintile Q1 averages +2.5, Q5 −2.5); it is flipped here. (2) Aprender's derived parental-education variables combine the level reached with a "did they complete it?" item that 11-year-olds answer unreliably (at every level "not completed" scores higher than "completed"); we use the highest level reached instead. (3) ERCE's teacher attendance index (ASISP) is higher when pupils report that teachers miss classes, arrive late or leave early (correlation +0.55 to +0.82 with those items). (4) Aprender teacher links: sections with exactly one responding teacher teaching the subject (60% of tested pupils in language, 58% in maths), so the sample leans towards schools where the questionnaire was answered. (5) Pupil-reported class and school climate measures (fights, safety, discipline, teachers' behaviour) are averaged over the pupils of the class or school and partly reflect who those pupils are.

Supply side (Part A). Aprender 2025 public primary schools with 5+ tested pupils and at least one responding teacher; teacher and pupil items averaged over the school's respondents. Within-province gaps: school indicator on within-province SES fifth (base = richest) with province fixed effects and rural; links with results: school average score (mean of language and maths, pupil SD) on the indicator plus school SES and rural with province fixed effects, weighted by tested pupils, robust SEs. The share of the poor–rich gap "accounted for" sums (poorest − richest mean) × coefficient from a model with all indicators together. Province-level links: 24 province averages, holding average SES constant. ERCE: school × grade units, gaps adjusted for sector and rural, links holding school SES, sector, rural and grade constant. Teacher questionnaires are answered by about 1.8 teachers per school, so school shares are noisy and links are understated.

Not causal. Teachers, directors and families sort across schools; the coefficients describe patterns, not effects.
