Supply problems are common and modestly linked to learning, but the ones we measure explain little of the poor–rich gap

Part A looks at the supply side in state schools: where it is weakest, whether it is worse in poorer schools, and whether it goes with lower results. Part B sets this against who the pupils are. Data: Aprender 2025 (grade 6 census), ERCE 2019 (grades 3 and 6), PISA 2018 (age 15) and MICS 2019–20 (urban households).

  • The measured supply problems do not explain why poor schools score lower. Within provinces the poorest fifth of state schools is SD behind the richest. With all the teacher and director items held constant the gap is SD. These are noisy self-reports, so this does not show that supply is irrelevant.
  • Teacher absence and punctuality are the clearest teacher-side problem that also hits poor pupils harder. In state schools, 47% of pupils in the poorest fifth say teachers miss class at least sometimes, against 36% in the richest (ERCE 2019). Only 3% of all pupils say "almost always". Lateness and leaving early also go with lower scores.
  • Leadership and classroom conditions have small, repeated links with learning, of 0.02–0.06 SD each: unclear goals and little feedback from directors (on the diagnostic tree page), directors new to the school, large classes, missing materials and devices.
  • Staffing differs far more between provinces than between poor and rich schools. Non-tenured teachers range from 19% to 90% by province. Within provinces the poor–rich gaps in tenure, substitutes and experience are 4 points or less, and tenure itself has no significant link with scores.
  • We cannot show that any statute rule changes these problems. Of 325 province-level tests, none survives adjustment for the number of tests. Rules that almost every province lacks cannot be tested this way: a career path in the classroom (1 of 24), performance-linked pay (1), an entry exam (1), principals choosing staff (0) and results in ratings (0).

Part A — the supply side: teachers, time in class, leadership and resources

A1. Which supply factors go with lower results? Regression across public schools (Aprender 2025)

What is estimated. Column (1) is one regression per factor and column (2) is one regression with all of them, across up to public primary schools.

  • Outcome (left-hand side): the school's average grade-6 score (mean of language and maths), in standard deviations of pupil scores. 1 SD ≈ 77 points in language and 92 in maths on the Aprender scale, so 0.10 SD ≈ 8–9 Aprender points.
  • Supply factor (right-hand side): a share from 0 to 1, e.g. the share of the school's responding teachers who are substitutes, or 0/1 for the director.
  • Coefficient: the expected difference in the school's average score between a school where none and a school where all of its respondents report the factor. Negative = lower results.
  • Held constant in every column: the school's average pupil SES and its square, rural location, and province (schools are only compared with schools in the same province).
  • Column (1): each factor in its own regression. Column (2): all teacher and director factors in one regression, so each coefficient is net of the other factors.

Weighted by tested pupils; robust standard errors in parentheses. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001. In column (1) blanks are missing and stars use p-values adjusted for the number of factors tested (Benjamini–Hochberg). Column (2) mean-fills blanks with a flag and uses unadjusted p-values. Associations, not effects.

Reading. The links are small next to the poor–rich gap of about half an SD.

  • Classroom conditions are the largest: frequent interruptions (−0.07), classes too big (−0.06), no devices (−0.06), missing materials (−0.05) and poor furniture (−0.04). All five remain significant with the other factors included.
  • Time in class: suspensions (−0.04, also in column 2) and too few hours (−0.03, not in column 2). Too few class days is not significant.
  • Staff stability: teachers new to teaching or to the school (−0.03 each) and directors new to the school (−0.02). None is significant in column (2). A second teaching job is −0.02.
  • No significant link: substitute or non-tenured status, university degree, language training, a job outside education, director tenure.
  • Pupil reports are shown as a check only. "Teachers do not explain" and noise look large (−0.42), but caring for family is larger (−0.65), so these items pick up who the pupils are.

Each coefficient compares a school where every respondent reports the factor with one where none does. The typical difference between two schools is a fraction of it.

Where are these factors worse? Poorest vs richest public schools

Share of schools' respondents reporting each factor, in the poorest and richest fifth of public schools by average pupil SES (national fifths). "Difference within the same province" compares the poorest with the richest fifth of public schools inside each province, holding rural location constant, in percentage points (standard error in parentheses); red shading = worse in poorer schools.

Reading. Within a province the staffing gaps between the poorest and richest state schools are 4 points or less. The larger gaps are in classroom internet, school internet, devices and materials (6 to 9 points). Teacher training and class size are slightly better in poor schools. Together the teacher and director factors reduce the score gap from to SD.

Reading. Only two factors survive that adjustment: missing teaching materials and no internet in class, and both also hold without CABA. Too few class days, multiple jobs and untenured directors are significant only before adjustment and lose significance without CABA.

The descriptive differences are large. Patagonia has unstable staffing and lost time: in Santa Cruz 90% of pupils' teachers are not tenured, 66% are substitutes, 47% report too few class days and 51% of pupils have a director reporting suspensions as a problem. In Tierra del Fuego the figures are 79%, 53%, 50% and 81%, and in Neuquén 85%, 58%, 26% and 31%. The poorer north lacks resources: no devices for 79% in Chaco, 72% in Formosa and 69% in Tucumán and Salta, and no classroom internet for 87% in Tucumán and 81% in Salta. Non-tenured directors are the norm: below 50% only in CABA (41%) and San Luis (42%).

A3. Absence, lateness and materials are where poor state schools fall behind (ERCE 2019)

ERCE 2019, state schools, grades 3 and 6 pooled. Poorest and richest are fifths of state schools by average family SES, and the gap holds rural and grade constant. The link is the pupil-level difference in SD from none to all, holding own SES, school SES and its square, rural and grade constant (five plausible values, errors clustered by school). Stars use p-values adjusted for the 14 tests.

Reading. Pupils in the poorest fifth are more likely to say teachers miss class (47% vs 36%), arrive late (29% vs 18%) or leave early (18% vs 12%), and all three go with lower results. The absence link holds in grade 6 and not in grade 3, lateness the reverse, and leaving early holds in both. Insufficient materials is far more common in poor schools (73% vs 45%), but its link with scores is not significant after adjustment. A day of four hours or less is common everywhere (67% vs 63%). Inexperienced teachers go with lower scores (−0.16) and are not significantly more common in poor schools.

A4. What households report: poorer children are more likely to lose class days (MICS 2019–20)

Children aged 7–14 attending school, urban Argentina; 95% confidence intervals. Missed class because the teacher was absent, and school closed by a teacher strike, in the last 12 months. All sectors, so part of the gradient is state versus private schools.

A6. Do provinces with different statute rules have different supply problems?

Statutes vary only between provinces, so each test has 17–24 observations. These tests cannot show how much a statute could change: a rule may matter in every province without explaining the differences between them. The same holds for a rule that is missing everywhere: the classroom career path, performance pay, entry exam, principal selection of staff and results in ratings are absent from the tables below for that reason, not because they do not matter.

Regression table: each statute rule as a 0/1 dummy

Each cell is a separate regression across the provinces coded on that rule. Outcome: the province's share with the supply problem, in percentage points (0–100). Regressors: the rule (1 = in the statute; numeric rules split at the cut shown) and province average SES. Coefficient: difference in percentage points between provinces with and without the rule, at the same SES; standard error in parentheses. Headings show provinces with the rule / coded. Rules with fewer than three provinces on either side are not tested.

Reading.

  • No rule or set of rules has a detectable link once the number of tests is allowed for.
  • This is weak evidence either way. With this many provinces a rule would typically need to move a problem by about percentage points to be detected reliably even in a single test.
  • The few stable cells are mostly not credible mechanisms. An age cap on entry going with no internet in schools identifies a group of provinces and is not an effect of the age rule.
  • Some cells match a mechanism but are fragile. Full-post appointment (D6) goes with 18 points fewer teachers holding a second post, and this is lost without CABA. A longer wait for substitutes (D1) goes with 13 points fewer substitutes, and this is lost without the four national-statute provinces.
  • The four national-statute provinces are not independent observations. Three share almost identical codes, so each result is also shown without all four.
  • For the memo: argue reforms from mechanisms and from research on them, and do not claim that these comparisons show that statutes work or that they do not.

B4. The predictors that matter, compared across datasets

Effects in standard deviations of the score (0.1 SD ≈ 10 PISA/ERCE points ≈ a third of a school year), each holding all other predictors constant. Bold = significant at 5%. Green = higher scores, red = lower. Blank = not measured in that dataset.

How to read the comparison

  • Family factors repeat across all three assessments and across ages: repetition, preschool, books, indigenous background and the maths gender gap are the most stable findings in the report.
  • Peer SES (the school's average family background) is the strongest school-level predictor everywhere; its units differ by dataset (Aprender's index has a wider spread), so compare signs and significance, not sizes.
  • Rural schools score slightly higher once family background is held constant: the raw urban–rural gap is a family-background gap.
  • Teacher contract, experience, degree and training are close to zero in Aprender (about 350,000 pupils, so even 0.02 SD is significant) and mostly not significant in ERCE; the exceptions are teachers with 5 years or less of experience in grade-3 maths (−0.18 SD) and a teaching qualification in grade-6 maths (+0.45 SD; few teachers lack one). Teacher absence and lateness is significant in three of the four ERCE models.
  • Within a model full of related measures, single coefficients can take unexpected signs (for example, home internet or a computer in Aprender, child work including chores in ERCE grade 6). Read them as "holding the other assets constant", not as the effect of providing internet.

B7. What the demand side means for the statute

  • The statute levers that work through contract status, degrees or training hours are unlikely, on this evidence, to move learning on their own: these teacher characteristics predict little once family and school are taken into account.
  • The teacher factor with the most consistent link is whether teachers are in the classroom (absence, lateness, leaving early), and it is worse in poor schools. Strike closures are also more common for poor children, though we cannot link them to scores. Statute rules on leave, substitution speed and attendance (dimension D) are the closest levers.
  • Because who the pupils are (family and peer SES) explains far more than any school or teacher variable, any comparison of provinces or schools should adjust for intake, as the province explorer's SES-expected measure does.
  • All results are associations in cross-sections: they describe who scores higher, not what would happen if a factor changed.

B8. Methods and caveats

Models. One weighted linear regression per dataset × grade × subject, outcome standardised to mean 0 and SD 1 (weighted, within the analysed sample). All four groups of predictors enter together; categorical predictors as dummies against a base; missing answers kept as their own category (numeric: mean-filled plus a missing flag) so that no pupil is dropped for one unanswered item. Geography includes province fixed effects (Aprender) or region fixed effects (PISA). Standard errors clustered by school. ERCE and PISA: every plausible value estimated separately and combined with Rubin's rules (school-clustered errors approximate the official replicate-weight errors). Weights: Aprender subject expansion weights; ERCE normalised weight WS; PISA final student weight.

Data notes. (1) Aprender 2025's pupil SES index (indice) is coded higher = poorer in the source (quintile Q1 averages +2.5, Q5 −2.5); it is flipped here. (2) Aprender's derived parental-education variables combine the level reached with a "did they complete it?" item that 11-year-olds answer unreliably (at every level "not completed" scores higher than "completed"); we use the highest level reached instead. (3) ERCE's teacher attendance index (ASISP) is higher when pupils report that teachers miss classes, arrive late or leave early (correlation +0.55 to +0.82 with those items). (4) Aprender teacher links: sections with exactly one responding teacher teaching the subject (60% of tested pupils in language, 58% in maths), so the sample leans towards schools where the questionnaire was answered. (5) Pupil-reported class and school climate measures (fights, safety, discipline, teachers' behaviour) are averaged over the pupils of the class or school and partly reflect who those pupils are.

Supply side (Part A). Aprender 2025 state primary schools with 5 or more tested pupils (11,087). Each statistic uses the schools that answered that item, and blanks are missing. Teacher and pupil items are averaged over the school's respondents and percentages are weighted by pupils. Within-province gaps: indicator on within-province SES fifth with province fixed effects and rural. Links: school mean score on the indicator, school SES, its square, rural and province fixed effects. The change in the poor–rich gap comes from adding all teacher and director items together (blanks mean-filled with a flag). ERCE: state schools, pupil level. About 1.8 teachers answer per school, so school shares are noisy and links are likely understated.

Not causal. Teachers, directors and families sort across schools; the coefficients describe patterns, not effects.
