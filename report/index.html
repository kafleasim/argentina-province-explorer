Why is learning low and unequal in Argentina?

Step 1: the possible explanations, drawn before looking at our data. Step 2: what our data say about each one.

Diagnostic treeRegressions and findings

Step 1. The diagnostic tree

The method follows DEV-101 (class 2): set the objective, ask why it is not met, list rival explanations, arrange them with models, use evidence to narrow them to the binding constraints, and only then choose remedies. Step 1 stops before the evidence, which is Step 2 further down this page. The branches come from how the Argentine system works and from the research literature. In Step 1 none is ranked, and none uses the estimates in this report.

Read down: each mechanism is a possible reason for the box above it, and the shaded box beneath it is the deeper cause. Select a box to jump to its model and tests. "Can a statute reach it" describes the policy instrument and says nothing about whether the constraint binds.

The Argentine setting behind the branches

  • Provinces run schools and employ teachers. Primary schools were transferred in 1978 (Law 21.809) and secondary schools from 1991–92 (Law 24.049). The nation sets goals, assesses and co-finances.
  • The career rules are old. Most provincial statutes descend from the 1958 national statute (Law 14.473). Neuquén, Río Negro, Santa Cruz and Tierra del Fuego still use it.
  • School results are restricted. Article 97 of the National Education Law (26.206) has been applied to prevent publication of results by school.
  • Financing has changed. The 2026 budget law (27.798, art. 30) repealed the article that set the target of 6% of GDP for education, and Decree 341/2025 narrowed the nation's role in teacher wage bargaining.
  • Strike rules are contested. The 2026 labour reform (Law 27.802) declared schooling an essential service with 75% minimum cover. A court suspended this in April 2026; we have not confirmed its status since.

Branches that feed each other

  • Vacancies, absence and fragmented posts (S1.1, S2.4, S2.6): each leave creates a substitute post, which creates another vacancy.
  • Macro instability works on both sides: it erodes pay and provokes strikes (S2.3, S2.5) while cutting household resources (D2.2).
  • Supply failures can look like weak demand: lost days and poor teaching drive exit and disengagement (D2.3, D3.2).
  • Missing information protects bad equilibria: if neither parents nor ministries see learning, neither asks for change (S1.3, D3.1).

When is a branch a priority for the Model Statute?

When both elasticities from class are large: (1) learning responds when the constraint is relaxed, and (2) the constraint responds to a change in the statute. A constraint can bind and still be a poor target for a statute (poverty, for example), and a rule can be easy to change without mattering for learning. Remedies must then pass the political, administrative and fiscal tests. If one fails, return to the tree and work another node.

Research is cited from abstracts and bibliographic records, not full texts. Studies from other countries support the mechanism, not its importance in Argentina. The tree was drafted after this report's analysis had been run, so the group should challenge it for missing branches.

Step 2. Do these problems show up in our data?

5 of the 20 branches look like problems, 10 exist with an unclear effect on learning, 1 does not show up as hypothesised, and 4 cannot be judged with our data.

  • Problems a teacher statute can reach directly: teacher absence and punctuality (S2.4) and school leadership (S3.1).
  • Problems that need other instruments: materials and classroom conditions (S3.4), early readiness (D2.1), and the cost of staying in school (D2.2).
  • Common, but not shown to harm learning: provisional posts (S1.1). This is central to a statute, so the memo should argue it on grounds of stability and administration.
  • Not supported as drawn: less experienced or less stable teachers concentrated in poor schools (S2.6). Within provinces those gaps are 4 percentage points or less.

None of this shows that a constraint binds. That needs the two elasticities: learning to the constraint, and the constraint to the statute. These data give an association for the first and nothing for the second.

How to read the numbers

  • SD is a standard deviation of pupils' scores. A coefficient of −0.05 means that a school where all responses report the problem scores 0.05 SD lower than one where none do, with similar pupil SES, the same rural or urban setting and, in Aprender, the same province.
  • For scale: the poorest fifth of state schools scores 0.49 SD below the richest fifth in the same province. Most supply links are a tenth of that or less. With all the teacher and director items held constant the gap is still 0.48 SD.
  • Pupils' reports about teaching are not used for verdicts. They have large coefficients (about −0.4), but so does "I care for family most days" (-0.65), which has nothing to do with teaching. They are probably picking up differences between pupils.
  • The exception is absence and punctuality (S2.4), which pupils observe directly. We checked it by scoring one random half of each school against the other half's reports, and the link remains. The leadership and materials links also remain after controlling for preschool, repetition, care work, seasonal absence and blank answers.
  • Coverage differs. Unless stated, percentages are pupil-weighted, for state-school grade 6, Aprender 2025. ERCE is state schools in 2019, grades 3 and 6. MICS is urban only, all sectors. Teacher answers are averaged over the school's respondents.
  • Verdicts are our judgement from the three questions on each card. They were set after seeing the results.

S1. Education agencies manage the system poorly

S1.1 Posts are filled late and provisionallyExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
Provisional staffing is common. In 2025 34% of state primary posts were interim, up from 25% in 2019 (25% to 32% without Buenos Aires province, whose series jumps in 2023). 76% of pupils have a director who is not tenured. Only 1.1% of posts are vacant.
Is it worse for poorer children?
Barely. The within-province gap in non-tenured teachers is +1 point. The differences between provinces are much larger (19% to 90%).
Is it linked to learning?
Not clearly: not tenured -0.015 SD, substitute -0.010 SD, neither significant after adjusting for multiple tests.
What can we say?
Posts are filled, but provisionally. We cannot show that this lowers learning.
What we would still need
Days to fill a post, and contest dates and backlogs, by province.

S1.2 Money does not reach where learning is producedExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
The symptoms exist: 36% of pupils have a director who says the school lacks the resources it needs. June 2026 gross pay runs from 71 in Mendoza to 193 in Neuquén (national average = 100).
Is it worse for poorer children?
Yes. Within a province the poorest fifth of schools is +11 points more likely to lack resources.
Is it linked to learning?
Slightly: lacking resources -0.020 SD. We have no spending data to link.
What can we say?
Resources are short where children are poorer. Whether the cause is allocation or the size of the budget is unknown.
What we would still need
Spending per pupil by province and school, split into salary and non-salary.

S1.3 Nobody answers for learningExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
Partly. 21% of pupils are in schools that did not receive the Aprender 2023 report or do not know, and 52% have a director who reviews learning data less than monthly. Among schools answering on the report, 90% say they made an improvement plan from it.
Is it worse for poorer children?
No. Directors of poor schools are, if anything, more likely to review data (gap in not reviewing: -6 points).
Is it linked to learning?
Mixed: no report -0.020 SD, not reviewing data -0.005 SD (not significant).
What can we say?
Schools say they receive and use results. Whether anyone is held to them is not observed.
What we would still need
What follows when a school or province has poor results.

S2. Teachers are ineffective

S2.1 Teachers start with weak skillsCannot tell

Does it exist?
Directors think so: in ERCE 2019 35% of pupils have a director who says half or more of the teachers need subject-knowledge training, and 31% one who reports a lack of teachers able to teach the subjects.
Is it worse for poorer children?
Not in subject knowledge as directors judge it (41% poorest fifth, 41% richest).
Is it linked to learning?
Paper qualifications say little: no university degree -0.006 SD, and initial training under four years goes with better results (+0.023 SD). The directors' judgement goes with lower scores (-0.12 SD).
What can we say?
We have opinions and credentials, with no measure of what teachers know.
What we would still need
A teacher knowledge test, or exam scores at entry.

S2.2 Skills do not improve on the jobExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
Partly. 32% had no maths training in three years and 56% none on mixed-ability classes. 18% say the director rarely visits class and gives feedback. In ERCE, 66% of pupils have a director who says half or more of the teachers need training for different learning paces.
Is it worse for poorer children?
No. Training and feedback are slightly better in poor schools (gaps -4 points and -6 points).
Is it linked to learning?
Small: no maths training -0.019 SD, no language training +0.004 SD (not significant), no feedback -0.032 SD.
What can we say?
Most teachers get some training. Its quality looks like the question, and we cannot observe it.
What we would still need
Content, length and practical component of courses, and classroom observations.

S2.3 Effort is lowCannot tell

Does it exist?
Not measured. The nearest proxy is the 30% of pupils whose director reports a lack of committed teachers (ERCE 2019).
Is it worse for poorer children?
That opinion is far more common in poor schools: 35% against 13%.
Is it linked to learning?
The opinion has no link to scores (-0.003 SD).
What can we say?
We cannot say effort is low, and should not claim it in the memo.
What we would still need
Observed time on task, or a change in incentives or appraisal that we can evaluate.

S2.4 Teachers are absentLikely a problem

Does it exist?
Yes, occasional rather than constant. 44% of state-school pupils say teachers miss class sometimes or more, but only 3% say almost always (ERCE 2019). 37% of urban children aged 7–14 lost class to teacher absence in a year (MICS 2019–20).
Is it worse for poorer children?
Yes. ERCE: 47% poorest fifth, 36% richest. MICS, all sectors: 43% against 24%.
Is it linked to learning?
Yes. Per 10 points more pupils reporting it: absence -0.021 SD, lateness -0.032, leaving early -0.055.
What can we say?
The clearest teacher-side problem that a statute reaches. It is pupil-reported, from 167 schools in 2019. The absence link holds in grade 6 and not grade 3, lateness the reverse, and leaving early holds in both. After controls for pupils' own absence, work and blank answers, lateness and leaving early remain significant and absence is borderline (p = 0.065).
What we would still need
Administrative leave and cover records. We cannot separate justified leave from weak monitoring.

S2.5 Classes are lost to strikesExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
Very common: 59% of urban children aged 7–14 had school closed by a strike in a year (MICS 2019–20). Today 8% of pupils have a director who calls suspended classes, for any reason, a serious or moderate problem.
Is it worse for poorer children?
Yes: 69% against 37%, which partly reflects state versus private schools. Suspensions are concentrated in Patagonia.
Is it linked to learning?
Suspensions -0.039 SD. Strikes themselves cannot be linked to scores in our data.
What can we say?
Exposure is large. The damage is documented by Jaume & Willén (2019) and untested by us.
What we would still need
Days lost by province and year.

S2.6 Teachers are in the wrong schools or spread thinNot as hypothesised

Does it exist?
Spread thin, yes: 43% also work at another institution. Sorted away from poor schools, no.
Is it worse for poorer children?
Hardly. Within provinces the poorest fifth has +4 points more novices, +4 points more substitutes and a gap of under 1 point in multiple job holding.
Is it linked to learning?
Small: novice -0.028 SD, new to the school -0.027, another job in education -0.016.
What can we say?
Sorting on the traits we observe cannot explain much of the poor–rich gap. A hardship bonus justified by that gap has weak support here.
What we would still need
Teacher effectiveness, which may be sorted even if tenure and experience are not. Transfer requests.

S3. Schools perform poorly

S3.1 Leadership is weakLikely a problem

Does it exist?
Yes, in stability and time. 76% of pupils have a director who is not tenured, 76% one with five years or less at the school, and 87% one doing administration every day.
Is it worse for poorer children?
No. It is general (gap in non-tenure +1 point).
Is it linked to learning?
Consistently but modestly: unclear goals -0.049 SD, no feedback -0.032, director rarely observes class -0.019, director new -0.021. Non-tenure alone is not significant (-0.013).
What can we say?
A plausible statute lever. The evidence points to what directors do and how long they stay more than to tenure as such.
What we would still need
How directors were selected and when, and their authority over staff.

S3.2 Teaching does not match the child's levelExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
Children are behind: 30% read at level II or lower in grade 3 (Aprender 2024) and 24% in state schools are below basic in grade 6 maths (2025). 61% of pupils are in schools without standardised diagnostic tests and 52% without organised recovery classes.
Is it worse for poorer children?
Children are further behind in poor schools, but those schools get more recovery classes (gap in having none: -14 points).
Is it linked to learning?
Not shown: no diagnostic test -0.005 SD (not significant). Schools without recovery score higher (+0.018), probably because remediation goes to weak schools.
What can we say?
The symptom is certain. That teaching is pitched at the wrong level is not observed.
What we would still need
What is taught compared with the level each child has reached.

S3.3 The school day is shortExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
Yes in 2019: 70% of pupils had a day of four hours or less (ERCE, before the extra-hour programme). In 2025 16% of pupils' teachers say daily hours are too few.
Is it worse for poorer children?
Slightly in 2019 (67% against 63%). No gap in the 2025 teacher reports.
Is it linked to learning?
Mixed: short day -0.07 SD (not significant), too few hours -0.030 SD.
What can we say?
Common, with a weak link in our data.
What we would still need
Actual hours by school since the 2022 extra-hour programme.

S3.4 Places and materials are shortLikely a problem

Does it exist?
Materials and conditions, yes: 35% lack teaching materials, 33% have poor furniture, 61% no classroom internet. Primary places, no: 0.1% of urban 6–11 year olds never attended. Preschool is the exception (15% of ages 3–5).
Is it worse for poorer children?
Yes: gaps of +6 points for materials and +9 points for internet.
Is it linked to learning?
Yes, among the largest supply links: class too big -0.064 SD, no materials -0.051, poor furniture -0.041.
What can we say?
A real problem that mostly needs budget instruments, outside the statute.
What we would still need
An infrastructure census and spending on inputs.

D1. Returns to education are low

D1.1 Schooling does not lead to good jobsCannot tell

Does it exist?
Returns are not measured. Final-year pupils behave as if study pays: 81% plan further study and 63% plan to work (Aprender 2024, more than one answer allowed).
Is it worse for poorer children?
Plans differ: further study 71% poorest fifth, 90% richest.
Is it linked to learning?
Not testable. These pupils have already stayed to the final year, so they are a selected group.
What can we say?
No sign of general disbelief in schooling. Actual returns are unknown.
What we would still need
Wages by education from the household survey (EPH).

D1.2 The diploma says little about skillsExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
The precondition exists: 54% of pupils in the last year of secondary are below basic in maths (Aprender 2024).
Is it worse for poorer children?
Completion differs: 42% of the poorest urban 20–24 year olds finished secondary, 87% of the richest.
Is it linked to learning?
Not testable: we do not see whether employers discount the diploma.
What can we say?
A diploma does not guarantee basic skills. Whether that weakens demand is unknown.
What we would still need
Pay by diploma at a given skill level.

D2. Household resources are low

D2.1 Children arrive unready to learnLikely a problem

Does it exist?
Yes. Gaps are large early: 44% of grade 3 pupils in the poorest schools read at level II or lower, against 13% in the richest (Aprender 2024).
Is it worse for poorer children?
Yes, and preschool repeats it: 18% of the poorest urban 3–5 year olds never attended, 4% of the richest.
Is it linked to learning?
Yes: no preschool -0.27 SD in grade 6 maths, comparing pupils in the same school with similar SES.
What can we say?
A major problem outside the statute. Grade 3 is after entry, so part of this gap may come from early schooling.
What we would still need
A measure of skills at school entry.

D2.2 Families cannot afford time in schoolLikely a problem

Does it exist?
Yes. 13% of urban 15–17 year olds are out of school, and 28% of final-year pupils work five hours or more a week (own account, for someone else or in a family business).
Is it worse for poorer children?
Strongly: out of school 26% poorest, 1% richest. Absence for families' seasonal work is a problem for 32% in the poorest state primaries against 10%.
Is it linked to learning?
Yes: work -0.037 SD and 20 or more absences -0.083 (same school and SES). Seasonal absence -0.061.
What can we say?
The steepest poor–rich gradient we see, mainly for attainment. It needs transfers and scholarships more than teacher rules.
What we would still need
Income shocks and who receives transfers.

D2.3 Those who can afford it leave state schoolsExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
The segregation exists: 62% of the richest fifth of grade 6 pupils are in private schools.
Is it worse for poorer children?
By construction: only 7% of the poorest fifth are.
Is it linked to learning?
The private advantage in maths falls from 0.58 to 0.19 SD once own and peer SES are held constant. The effect on those who stay is untested.
What can we say?
Exit is large and is mostly sorting. Whether it harms state schools is unknown.
What we would still need
Moves from state to private schools, and whether they follow strikes.

D3. Information and beliefs are poor

D3.1 Parents overestimate what children learnCannot tell

Does it exist?
Not measured: none of our datasets asks parents what they think their child has learned.
Is it worse for poorer children?
Unknown.
Is it linked to learning?
Not testable. A related clue is that 58% of grade 6 pupils who are below basic in maths say they are good at it, but these are pupils' beliefs.
What can we say?
Plausible and untested.
What we would still need
A parent survey set against the child's result.

D3.2 Pupils underrate returns or their own abilityExists, effect unclear

Does it exist?
Mixed. 24% of grade 6 pupils at satisfactory or above underrate their maths, but 58% of those below basic overrate it.
Is it worse for poorer children?
Aspirations are lower among the poor: 50% of grade 6 pupils in the poorest state schools expect secondary at most, against 27% (ERCE 2019).
Is it linked to learning?
Not testable: beliefs and results affect each other.
What can we say?
Low aspirations exist among poorer pupils. Underrating ability is not the general pattern.
What we would still need
Perceived against actual returns.
